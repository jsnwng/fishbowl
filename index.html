October 14 2026-10-14

I was laid off from Netflix on October 14, two months shy of a seven-year tenure. I spent it building internal UIs for the people inside.

All of it lived behind the company firewall, in systems no one outside will ever see. A few of those projects felt like the future. Plenty of them felt like plumbing. Plumbing is the kind of work nobody notices until it breaks. When you do it right, nothing happens. I spent a lot of those years making sure nothing happened, and I got pretty good at it.

The LinkedIn messages started almost right away. Some were referrals, others offered words of encouragement and invitations to catch up. People asked what was next, then told me I'd be fine. Maybe. Then one friend checked in to see how I was doing and asked if I could help with something: how do you build a web game?

Renderers 2026-10-16

A web game has a lot of moving parts, and I'd never built one. So I started where my friend was in their project: figuring out how to draw it. There's no shortage of 2D renderers, or of benchmark posts comparing them, but each post tests something different on different hardware, and some are written by the people who make the library. All of that can be overwhelming, even paralyzing, for someone just starting out. I wanted numbers I could reproduce, measured against what my friend actually needed to build, so I built a test.

The test is fishbowl, an 8-bit aquarium. It's a contrived example: nobody, my friend included, is building a fishing game. An aquarium is just an easy way to put a lot of small sprites on screen at once, which is most of what a 2D game asks of its renderer. The same seeded fish swim through the same frames while six renderers take turns drawing them: raw Canvas2D, raw WebGL, PixiJS, Konva, Two.js and p5.js. Each renderer is a small adapter behind the same interface, and one loop drives all of them, so the renderer is the only thing that changes between runs.

The fish move one fixed step per frame from a seeded random number generator, so every renderer is handed identical frames. The ramp test starts at 500 fish and adds a quarter more every two seconds, until the slowest 5% of frames take longer than 18 ms. Then it reports the last count that held.

On my machine, an RTX 3080 Ti with a 240 Hz display, WebGL held about 166,000 fish. At that point the bottleneck was the fish simulation, not the drawing. PixiJS held about 28,000, Canvas2D and p5.js about 4,700, Konva about 1,500, and Two.js stayed under 500.

Despite the numbers, my friend went with PixiJS. It's far easier to understand and review than raw WebGL, and 28,000 sprites is more than most games will ever need. The benchmark answered which renderer is fastest, but that was never really the question. The question was what their project needed: how much it had to draw, and who would be building and maintaining it. Once that was clear, the choice was easy. In my experience that's true of most software projects. Building is rarely the hard part. Agreeing on what you're building is.

Players 2026-10-17

Drawing was only half of it. The next question from my friend was the natural one: "So how do I make it a game?" Neither of us had built one before, so this part was as much about learning as testing and explaining it to friends along the way. The first thing I had to understand was how game AI usually works. Most enemies and bots in games aren't trained models at all. They're heuristics: rules a developer writes by hand, like a state machine that switches between patrol, chase and attack, or a list of priorities that picks the most useful action. Designers often make them worse on purpose, with reaction delays or missed shots, because an opponent with perfect aim isn't fun to play against. Learned models do show up, like the driving AI in Forza and Gran Turismo, but they're the exception.

That raised a question of my own. Small AI models can now run in the browser, on the player's own machine. Could a browser game hand some of its decisions to one, and what would that cost? To find out, I needed a game where decisions matter, so the bowl got a fisherman. Like the aquarium, it's a stand-in rather than a game anyone is building: small enough to understand completely, with just enough rules that the decisions count.

Anything that fishes is a player: you, a bot following hand-written rules, or eventually a trained model. Every player sees the same bowl and answers the same way, with cast, reel or wait. The game is headless and seeded, so the same seed and the same moves always produce the same run, and every player faces the same fish.

The rules are there so that skill pays off. A splash scares nearby fish, so you have to cast where a fish is going rather than where it is. A nibble lasts about 0.4 seconds before the fish takes the bait. Bait goes stale after a few seconds, so waiting in one spot stops working.

Simple bots turned out to be the best way to test the rules. In my first version, a bot that casts in one spot and waits caught 42 fish against 45 for the hand-written heuristic, which meant aiming barely mattered. Once that was fixed, the heuristic started landing fish the moment its hook hit the water. The fish swam in perfectly straight lines, so its aim was simple math. A real game would make the bot worse on purpose. For a fair test I made the fish less predictable instead: they now change direction and depth from time to time, and leading one is an estimate rather than a certainty. Over 20 seeds the heuristic averages about 14 catches, cast and wait about 7, and random about 1.

Thinking time 2026-10-18

A model doesn't answer instantly, so the game now lets a player take its time. Players are asked at decision points, whenever the line changes state and every half second otherwise, which comes to about 200 times per run. In real time the game keeps moving while a player thinks, so a late answer can miss its moment. In turn based the game waits, which measures the quality of each decision on its own.

To see what delay alone costs, I ran the heuristic in real time with every answer held back by a fixed amount. Same decisions, 20 seeds:

Time to decideCatches
InstantAbout 14
100 msAbout 12
250 msAbout 7
500 ms or moreAlmost none

A nibble only lasts 0.4 seconds, so anything slower than that loses every fish it hooks. A language model running locally will usually take longer than that to respond. My guess is that a model is better suited to slower choices, like where to cast and when to move on, while fixed code handles the reflexes. The next experiments will test that.

Next

I'm learning to train a small model on this game in Python. The plan is to port the game's simulation, check that it matches the browser version frame for frame, teach a network to copy the heuristic, and then use reinforcement learning to try to beat it. Along the way I'll be working out how to watch and measure a model while it trains. After that come language models running in the browser.
